# Moore's Law Explained Simply

## The Core Idea
In 1965, Intel co-founder **Gordon Moore** observed that the number of **transistors** (tiny electronic switches) on a computer chip **doubles roughly every two years**. Computers get faster and cheaper over time.

## Analogy
Imagine a city where the number of apartments in the same land area doubles every two years. You'd need to build smaller and smaller apartments to fit more in the same space. That's what engineers do with transistors.

Each step up is not "a bit more"; it's **exponential growth** (1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 ...).

## Real Examples
- **Intel 4004 (1971):** ~2,300 transistors
- **Pentium (1993):** ~3 million
- **Apple M1 Ultra (2022):** ~114 billion

## Why It Matters
- Smartphones today are more powerful than room-sized supercomputers of the past.
- Technology gets cheaper and more capable.

## Limits
- Transistors are approaching **atomic scale**, so shrinking is harder.
- Heat and cost are growing problems.
- The industry now focuses on **specialized chips (GPUs, AI chips)**, 3D stacking, and new materials.

## Summary Table

| Key Point | Details |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Gordon Moore (Intel co-founder) |

| **What** | Transistor count doubles about every 2 years |

| **Result** | Faster, smaller, cheaper electronics |
| **Example** | 2,300 transistors (1971) → billions (2020s) |
| **Challenges** | Atomic limits, heat, rising manufacturing costs |
| **Future** | Chip specialization, 3D design, new materials, quantum computing |

**Note:** Moore's Law isn't a law of physics. It's an observation and industry goal, and it's slowing down.
