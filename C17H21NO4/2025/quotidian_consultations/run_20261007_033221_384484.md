# Moore's Law Explained Simply

## The Core Idea
In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore observed that the number of **transistors** (tiny electronic switches) on a computer chip **doubles roughly every two years**. Chips get faster and cheaper over time.

## Everyday Analogy
Imagine a city where the number of houses doubles every 2 years on the same plot of land. To fit more in, each house must keep shrinking. That's what engineers do with transistors.

## Visualization

Transistors per chip (approximate, log-style growth):

```
Year   Chip              Transistors
1971   Intel 4004        2,300        ▏
1985   Intel 386         275,000      ██
1999   Pentium III       9.5 million  ████
2010   Core i7           1 billion    ██████
2020   Apple M1          16 billion   ████████
2023   Apple M2 Ultra    134 billion  ██████████
```

Each step is not +1 but **×2, ×2, ×2...** (exponential growth):

```
2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128 ...
```

After 20 doublings, you'd have about 1 million times more than you started with.

## Why It Matters
- Phones today are more powerful than room-sized supercomputers of the 1980s.
- Drives progress in AI, graphics, medicine, and more.

## Is It Still True?
It's **slowing down**. Transistors are now only a few atoms wide, so heat and quantum effects make shrinking harder. The industry is turning to new strategies:
- 3D chip stacking
- Specialized chips (GPUs, AI accelerators)
- New materials and designs

## Summary Table

| Key Point | Details |
|---|---|
| **Who** | Gordon Moore, 1965 (revised 1975) |
| **Claim** | Transistor count doubles about every 2 years |
| **Type of growth** | Exponential |
| **Result** | Faster, smaller, cheaper electronics |
| **Not a real law** | An observation and industry goal, not physics |
| **Current status** | Slowing due to physical limits |
| **Future paths** | 3D stacking, specialized chips, new materials |
