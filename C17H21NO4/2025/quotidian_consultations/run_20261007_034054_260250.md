# Moore's Law Explained Simply

## The Idea
In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore observed that the number of **transistors** (tiny electronic switches) on a computer chip **doubles roughly every two years**. More transistors generally means faster, cheaper, and more powerful computers.

## Everyday Analogy
Imagine a pizza that gets **cut into twice as many slices every two years**, yet the pizza stays the same size. The slices (transistors) keep getting smaller, so you can fit far more of them in the same space.

## Visualization

Transistors per chip (approximate, log-style growth):

```
Year   Transistors
1971   2,300        ▏
1981   100,000      ██
1991   1,000,000    ████
2001   40,000,000   ██████
2011   1,000,000,000 ████████
2021   50,000,000,000 ██████████████
```

Each step is not just a little taller; it's **many times bigger**. This is *exponential growth*: doubling over and over.

## A Quick Doubling Example
Start with 1 and double every 2 years:

| Years | Multiplier |
|-------|-----------|
| 2     | 2×        |
| 10    | 32×       |
| 20    | 1,024×    |
| 40    | ~1,000,000× |

## Why It Matters
- Smartphones today are more powerful than room-sized computers of the 1960s.
- Electronics get cheaper and better over time.

## Limits
Transistors are now only a few atoms wide, so physics (heat, quantum effects) and cost are slowing the pace. Engineers now rely on new designs, such as 3D stacking, specialized chips (GPUs, AI chips), and multiple cores.

## Summary Table

| Key Point | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Who** | Gordon Moore, 1965 (updated 1975) |
| **What** | Transistors per chip double about every 2 years |
| **Type of growth** | Exponential |
| **Effect** | Faster, smaller, cheaper electronics |
| **Example** | Intel 4004 (1971): 2,300 transistors; modern chips: tens of billions |
| **Nature** | An observation/trend, not a law of physics |
| **Current status** | Slowing due to atomic-scale limits |
| **Future paths** | 3D chips, specialized processors, quantum computing |
