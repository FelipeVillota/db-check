# Moore's Law Explained Simply

**The Core Idea:** Every ~2 years, the number of transistors on a computer chip doubles, making computers faster, cheaper, and smaller over time.

## Simple Analogy
Imagine you have a box of LEGO bricks representing computing power:
- 1970: 1,000 bricks fit in the box
- 1972: 2,000 bricks fit (same box size!)
- 1974: 4,000 bricks fit
- 1976: 8,000 bricks fit

The "box" (chip size) stays roughly the same, but engineers keep finding ways to pack in more and more tiny components.

## Real-World Impact
Think of it like this: if cars improved at the same rate as computers since 1970, a car today would:
- Cost pennies
- Go faster than the speed of light
- Fit in your pocket

## Why It's Slowing Down
Transistors are now approaching the size of **individual atoms** — you simply can't make them much smaller without hitting physical limits!

## Summary Table

| **Aspect** | **Details** |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | Observation that transistor count on chips doubles ~every 2 years |
| **Who proposed it** | Gordon Moore (Intel co-founder), 1965 |
| **Type** | Empirical observation, not a physical law |
| **Result** | Computers get faster, cheaper, smaller over time |
| **Time period** | Held strong from ~1970s–2010s |
| **Current status** | Slowing down due to physical/atomic limits |
| **Real-world effect** | Smartphones today have more power than 1960s supercomputers |
| **Key limitation** | Transistors can't shrink past atomic scale |
