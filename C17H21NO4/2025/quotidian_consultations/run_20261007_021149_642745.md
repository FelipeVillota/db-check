# Moore's Law Explained

## The Simple Idea
In 1965, Intel co-founder Gordon Moore observed that the number of **transistors** (tiny electronic switches) on a computer chip **doubles roughly every two years**, while cost per transistor falls.

Think of it like a city: every two years, you fit twice as many people into the same land area.

## Visualization

Transistors per chip (approximate, log-like growth):

```
Year   Transistors
1971   2,300         ▏
1980   30,000        ▎
1990   1,000,000     ▌
2000   40,000,000    ██
2010   1,000,000,000 ██████
2020   50,000,000,000 ████████████████
```

Doubling pattern:

```
2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128 ...
(each step ≈ 2 years)
```

After 20 years, that's 10 doublings, or about **1,000× more**.

## Real-World Example
- **1971 Intel 4004:** ~2,300 transistors
- **2020s chips (e.g., Apple M-series):** tens of billions

Your smartphone is far more powerful than the room-sized computers that guided Apollo missions.

## Current Status
Growth is slowing because transistors are approaching atomic scales, with heat and physics limits. The industry now relies on chiplets, 3D stacking, and specialized processors (GPUs, AI chips).

## Summary Table

| Key Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Originator** | Gordon Moore (1965, revised 1975) |
| **Statement** | Transistor count doubles about every 2 years |
| **Nature** | Observation/trend, not a law of physics |
| **Effects** | Faster, cheaper, smaller, more efficient electronics |
| **Examples** | 4004 (2,300) → modern chips (50B+) |
| **Limits** | Atomic size, heat, rising manufacturing costs |
| **Future** | Chiplets, 3D stacking, AI accelerators, possibly quantum computing |

**Bottom line:** Moore's Law explains why technology has improved exponentially for decades, though the pace is now changing.
